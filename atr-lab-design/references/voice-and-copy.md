# Voice and copy

How the Advanced Telerobotics Research Lab sounds, and the rules and templates for writing anything it publishes: headlines, slides, posters, captions, web pages, emails, recruiting posts, K-12 program copy, sponsor asks, award news and invitations. Kent State University's editorial style (AP plus university exceptions) governs, with a lab word list on top.

**Read this when** you are writing or editing any words the lab will show to someone: a headline, a paragraph, a caption, alt text, a call to action, an email or a whole page. Names, facts, proof points and boilerplates live in references/brand-foundation.md; read that first if the copy makes any claim about the lab.

**Looking for a boilerplate?** The canonical one-liner, 25-, 50- and 100-word versions, social bio and director bio are in references/brand-foundation.md §10. If another file quotes a different version, that section wins.

## 1. Voice

The lab's voice is **precise, curious, hands-on, welcoming and grounded**. These are the same five attributes, in the same order, as the canonical list in references/brand-foundation.md §6, which adds the "we are / we are not" pairs and two standing notes (always part of Kent State; persistent, not boastful, after competitions).

| Principle | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| **Precise**: names, venues, dates and numbers with units; nothing we cannot back up | "Selected as one of 11 finalists in the World Robot Summit Plant Disaster Prevention Challenge (2021)" | "Award-winning world-class robotics" |
| **Curious**: show the question before the answer | "How does a delivery robot tell you where it's about to go?" | "We present a novel framework for intent transparency." (fine in a paper, not in public copy) |
| **Hands-on**: real hardware, real students, real demos | "Students program a Unitree Go2 robot dog" (when true) | "Students engage with cutting-edge platforms" |
| **Welcoming**: a first-year student or a parent can follow it | "Teleoperation (controlling a robot from a distance)" | Undefined acronyms; "as everyone knows" |
| **Grounded**: robots extend and help people | "Robots that help people rather than replace them" | Robot-apocalypse jokes, fear hooks, "robots will take your job" |

Working rules that follow from the voice:

1. **Clarity over cleverness.** If a reader has to decode "tele-embodiment" in a headline, they are gone. Define it or show it.
2. **Outcome first, method second.** Lead with what the work lets people do; put the method in the second paragraph or on the project page.
3. **Specific over vague.** Replace "innovative platform" with the robot's name and what it does.
4. **The reader's words.** Parents say "robotics camp", not "K-12 STEM outreach program". Graduate applicants search "robotics Ph.D. Ohio".
5. **One idea per section, one call to action per piece.**
6. **Honest over sensational.** Keep every hedge in the source. Never upgrade "may" to "will", "in the lab" to "in the real world", "in simulation" to "on hardware", or "teleoperated" to "autonomous".
7. **Active and confident.** "We built" not "was developed". Cut "very", "really", "just", "actually".
8. **Person.** On the lab's own channels, the lab is "we" and the reader is "you". Press releases and formal bios use the third person. Never "the ATR" or "ATR" alone (references/brand-foundation.md §2).
9. **Exclamation marks:** none, except at most one in a congratulations post.

## 2. Tone by audience

The voice stays the same; the tone shifts with the reader. One piece, one primary audience.

| Audience | Goal of the copy | Register | Technical depth | Reading level (US grade) | Lead with | Avoid | Emoji | Sample line |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sponsors and industry** | A lab visit, then a conversation with the right Kent State office | Professional, concise | Medium; outcomes first | 9 to 11 | Their need, then the students and hardware that meet it | Promised terms, amounts or benefits the university has not approved | None | "Meet the students who already program Pepper, Unitree Go2 and Booster K1 robots." |
| **Funders and program managers** | A citable, current record of the work | Formal, exact | High | 11+ | Research question, outcomes, broader impacts | Overclaiming; a funder's name without the correct acknowledgment | None | "Outcomes from [award] are summarized at [project page]." |
| **Academic peers and reviewers** | Read, cite, reuse, collaborate | Academic, exact | High | As the venue requires | The contribution in one sentence | Hype words; demo video without speed and autonomy labels | None | "We present VendoBot, a projection-augmented delivery robot for improving intent transparency and motion legibility in service robots." |
| **Prospective graduate students** | Apply through Kent State and email the director a strong note | Collegial | Medium to high | 10 to 12 | Open topics and real platforms | Implied guaranteed funding, admission or co-authorship | None | "Work on Physical AI with humanoid and quadruped robots. Apply through Kent State by [deadline]." |
| **Prospective undergraduates** | Come to open lab or apply | Friendly, direct | Light to medium | 8 to 10 | What you will do in your first month | "You need experience" (unless true); jargon | 0 to 2 (Instagram) | "Build real robots before you graduate. Open lab is [day, time]." |
| **High school students** | Apply for the summer internship | Encouraging, clear | Light | 8 to 9 | A real research team, a real summer | Pressure, fake urgency, DMs | 0 to 1 | "High school students: spend your summer on a real robotics research team." |
| **Middle school students** (flyers and slides they see; enrollment goes through parents) | Get excited and tell a parent | Playful, concrete | Minimal | 5 to 6 | Something they will make | Anything they could read as a test; scare stories | 0 to 1 | "[Build a robot]. Teach it to [task]. [Show your family on the last day.]" (activities stay bracketed until the program plan is published) |
| **Parents and guardians** | Register, and trust the program | Warm, plain | Minimal; define every term | 6 to 8 | What their child will do; dates, cost, safety | Jargon; urgency aimed at children; a supervision claim before the program is registered | 0 to 1, at the end | "Your child will [build and program a robot] in a Kent State research lab this summer." |
| **Teachers and counselors** | Request a visit or share with students | Professional, practical | Light | 8 to 10 | What a visit looks like hour by hour | Vague offers | None | "We offer demos, learning sessions and workshops, and we can tailor a visit to your class." |
| **Media** (through Kent State media relations) | A story and an interview | Plain, quotable | Low | 8 | The finding or the human story | Jargon; claims beyond the paper | None | "Kent State researchers built a delivery robot that shows people where it's going next." (confirm with the project lead) |
| **Kent State leadership and UCM** | Retell the story; approve it | Plain, factual | Low to medium | 10 | The outcome for the university | Surprises; off-brand or non-compliant material | None | "[N] Northeast Ohio students did research in the lab this summer." (numbers from lab records) |

Reading levels are targets, not rules. Measure with a readability tool (for example the `textstat` Python package, `textstat.flesch_kincaid_grade(text)`, or the Hemingway editor).

## 3. Kent State editorial style

Kent State "closely follows the Associated Press Stylebook", with university exceptions that win where they apply (kent.edu/ucm/guide-university-style). The rules the lab hits most often:

### 3.1 Names and references

| Topic | Rule | Right | Wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| The university | "Kent State University" on first reference, then "Kent State" or "the university" (lowercase) | "Kent State University … Kent State … the university" | "KSU" in running copy; "Kent State campus" |
| KSU | Avoid in copy. Acceptable only in tightly spaced, tabulated copy or for Northeast Ohio audiences (kent.edu/ucm/n; the web guide says "avoid KSU"). University-issued hashtags such as #UndeniablyKSU are fine | "Kent State" | "KSU robotics lab" |
| The lab | "Advanced Telerobotics Research Lab" first, then "the lab"; "ATR Lab" only in display and tight space (references/brand-foundation.md §2) | "the Advanced Telerobotics Research Lab … the lab" | "the ATR", "ATR" alone as a second reference |
| Department | Official name capitalized first, lowercase after | "Department of Computer Science … the computer science department … the department" | "CS Dept.", "Computer Science Department" |
| College | "College of Sciences and Humanities", then "the college" | | "College of Arts and Sciences" (out of date) |
| Unit acronyms | Kent State does not use acronyms for units, even research centers ("Do not use RCET"). On a webpage only, after the full name, an acronym may appear in a headline or link where space is tight | | |
| Kent Campus | Always capitalized | "on the Kent Campus" | "on the Kent State campus" |
| People, first reference | Full name, then title after the name, lowercase | "Jong-Hoon Kim, associate professor of computer science and director of the Advanced Telerobotics Research Lab" | "Dr. Kim, Director of ATR" |
| People, later references | Last name only | "Kim said" | "Dr. Kim said", "Jong-Hoon said" |
| Academic title before a name | Avoid. Put the title after the name, lowercase. Capitalize only named or emeritus titles, as Kent State does ("Professor Emeritus of Physics"). Check titles in the catalog faculty directory (catalog.kent.edu/faculty-administrators/faculty/) | "Jong-Hoon Kim, associate professor of computer science" | "Associate Professor Jong-Hoon Kim" |
| "Dr." | Avoid in news-style copy (AP). On slides, posters and cards, the lab's display convention is "Ph.D." after the full name | "Jong-Hoon Kim, Ph.D." | "Dr. Jong-Hoon Kim, Ph.D." |
| Department heads | "chair" or "chairperson" | | "chairman" |
| Students | Name only with the student's written permission; year and major when useful | "[First Last], a senior computer science major" | A name in a photo caption without permission |
| Minors | Never name them. Photos only with a signed parent or guardian release. Full checklist: §6.5 | "a middle school participant" | a first and last name, a school next to a face |
| Class year | "first-year student", not "freshman" | | |
| Alumni | alumnus, alumna, alumni | "a 2024 lab alumnus" | "alum", "alums" |
| Degrees | Ph.D., M.S., bachelor's degree, master's degree | "a Ph.D. in computer science" | "PhD", "Masters degree" |

### 3.2 Punctuation and symbols

| Topic | Rule | Right | Wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Serial (Oxford) comma | **None** in a simple series; add one only when needed for clarity (kent.edu/ucm/c-d) | "telepresence, autonomy and AI" | "telepresence, autonomy, and AI" |
| Ampersand | Write "and". "&" only inside another organization's proper name, in web navigation and design elements, or in web titles and links for brevity. Never in unit names (kent.edu/ucm/b) | "Physical AI and Robotics" | "Physical AI & Robotics" (in copy) |
| Verbatim quotes | Keep the source's own punctuation, including its serial commas and "&", inside quotation marks | | |
| Em dash | Sparingly: at most one per page. Prefer a comma, colon or parentheses. AP sets it with a space on each side | "The lab has one rule — label the video." | Three dashes in a paragraph |
| Ranges | Hyphen, no spaces | "9 a.m.-noon", "Sept. 23-25" | "9am – 12pm" |
| Percent | Numerals and the % sign, no space (kent.edu/ucm/o-z) | "5%" | "5 percent", "5 %" |
| Ordinals | AP: spell out first through ninth in running copy; numerals from 10th. Kent State: never a superscript. "1st" and "2nd" (no superscript) only in tables, badges and display type | "first place", "second place", "10th"; badge: "1st place" | "1<sup>st</sup> place"; "1st place" in a sentence |
| Exclamation marks | See §1 rule 9 | | |
| Quotation marks | Periods and commas inside; titles of papers, talks and videos in quotation marks in running text | the paper "[Title]," | |

### 3.3 Numbers, dates, times and contact details

| Topic | Rule | Right | Wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Numbers (AP) | Spell out one through nine; numerals for 10 and up. Always numerals for ages, percentages, money, dimensions, dates, times and scores. Spell out a number that starts a sentence (except a year). Credit hours are always numerals (Kent State exception) | "three robots", "12 students", "ages 12 to 14", "3 credit hours" | "3 robots", "twelve students" |
| Money | "$2,000", "$1.5 million" | | "$2000.00", "2K" |
| Months with a date | Abbreviate Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov., Dec.; spell out March, April, May, June, July | "Sept. 23", "May 11-15", "Aug. 14" | "Sep. 23", "September 23rd" |
| Months without a date | Spell out | "January 2027", "spring 2027" | "Jan. 2027" |
| Year punctuation | Comma after the year mid-sentence | "On Sept. 23, 2026, the lab …" | |
| Days | Spelled out, not abbreviated, in running copy | "Friday, Oct. 2" | "Fri., 10/2" |
| Times | "9 a.m.", "9:30 a.m.", "noon", "midnight"; no ":00"; lowercase a.m. and p.m. with periods; add "ET" for online events | "9 a.m.-noon" | "9:00 AM", "12 p.m." |
| Phone | Hyphens, no parentheses | "330-672-9980" | "(330) 672-9980", "+1 (330) 672-9980" |
| Email, website | "email" is one word, lowercase. Print web addresses without "https://" and without a trailing slash; no link shorteners | "www.atr.cs.kent.edu" | "E-mail", "bit.ly/…" |

### 3.4 Capitalization and titles

- **Headlines, slide titles, poster section heads and buttons: sentence case.** Capitalize the first word and proper nouns only. Why: it is AP news style, it reads faster and it avoids inconsistent title-case decisions across a team. Right: "Students demo a delivery robot at open lab". Wrong: "Students Demo A Delivery Robot At Open Lab".
- **Titles of events, programs, courses, conferences and workshops** are capitalized as proper names; course titles take no quotation marks: "CS 43301 Software Development for Robotics", "2026 Summer Workshop", "National Robotics Week".
- **Paper titles** keep their published capitalization, in quotation marks in running text; reference lists follow the venue style.
- **All caps:** never for sentences, buttons or body copy (Kent State accessibility guidance: no long ALL CAPS). Short labels of one to four words may be *styled* as caps (references/typography.md); type them in sentence case in the source so screen readers do not spell them out.
- **Robot, project and system names** exactly as the lab writes them: TeleBot-3R, TeleBot-4R, VendoBot, Coffee Buddy, PepperKit, AARON, ImmersiFLY, Pepper, Unitree Go2, Booster K1, ROS 2.

### 3.5 Places

- "Kent, Ohio" in running text; "Kent, OH 44242" only in a full address; add "USA" for international audiences.
- "Northeast Ohio" (one word, capital N). The lab's homepage writes "North East Ohio"; don't copy it.
- Building: "Mathematical Sciences Building"; room: "Mathematical Sciences Building, Room [###]" (the lab's own room is unconfirmed; references/brand-foundation.md §11).
- Street: "Lefton Esplanade", as written.

### 3.6 Credits, citations and required lines

- **Kent State trademark line** on printed lab pieces that carry Kent State marks, small, preferably on the back: "Kent State University, Kent State and KSU are registered trademarks and may not be used without permission."
- **Citations:** Turabian when lab achievements appear in a general or mixed-discipline university list; the discipline's style (IEEE is usual in robotics and computer science) in the lab's own publication lists, posters and slides.
- **Funding acknowledgment:** name the funder and award number exactly as the award record gives them, and only after the PI confirms the award funded that work (`[Grant No.]` until then). For NSF-funded work, non-journal materials (web pages, flyers, posters, slides, releases, newsletters) carry the acknowledgment and the disclaimer. Current NSF award terms (every Grant General Conditions edition since Oct. 1, 2024, now July 31, 2026, Art. 28; also awards made on or after May 20, 2024 under the Research Terms and Conditions) read: "This material is based upon work supported by the U.S. National Science Foundation under award No. [No.]" and "Any opinions, findings and conclusions or recommendations expressed in this material are those of the author(s) and do not necessarily reflect the views of the U.S. National Science Foundation." Older awards follow the terms in their award letter (PAPPG 24-1 text, without "U.S.", and no logo clause). Current terms also require the NSF full-color logo on products from NSF-funded work, social posts and video included; the rules (portal files, 0.625 in minimum, never on business cards, email signatures or profile pictures, brand clearance) are in references/pr-events-outreach.md §18.2, and the logo slot and sizes in references/quad-charts.md, references/posters.md and references/presentations.md. Write "U.S. National Science Foundation" on first mention, then "NSF". Sponsor credit wording in general: §6.9.
- **Equal-opportunity or nondiscrimination statements:** never write your own. Paste the current wording from UCM, HR or the Office of General Counsel. The legacy recruitment line on kent.edu may be out of date.
- **Image credits:** every third-party photo needs a license and a credit. AI-generated images are captioned "Illustration" (or "Concept illustration" when they sit near real results), and their alt text starts with "Illustration:" (references/imagery.md, references/ai-image-pipeline.md).

### 4.3 Hype and AI-writing tells to cut

Cut on sight: "cutting-edge", "state-of-the-art" (name the method instead), "revolutionary", "groundbreaking", "game-changing", "world-class", "innovative" (say what is new), "novel" (in public copy), "leverage", "utilize", "seamless", "robust", "holistic", "transformative", "pivotal", "paramount", "delve", "at its core", "it's worth noting that", "in today's fast-paced world", "in the ever-evolving landscape of", "shed light on", "pave the way for", "a myriad of", "a plethora of", "furthermore" and "moreover" (use "also"), "in conclusion", the "whether you're an X, a Y or a Z" opener, and "it's not just X, it's Y". Claims of "first", "only" or "leading" need a source.

### 4.4 Writing about AI and autonomy

The lab now leads with Physical AI, and its public demos include a Pepper robot that answers through an LLM voice agent (PepperKit). Readers take AI claims literally, and children take them most literally of all, so these rules keep the copy honest.

| # | Rule | Right | Wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Concrete verbs for what the system does.** Say the operation, not a mental state | "detects people in its path", "plans a route", "answers using a large language model" | "understands", "knows", "feels", "wants", "thinks" |
| 2 | **Label control and footage every time a robot is shown.** Teleoperated, autonomous, semi-autonomous or scripted; live or recorded; playback speed | "Recorded demo, [2×] speed. The robot is [teleoperated from the next room]." | A sped-up autonomous-looking clip with no label |
| 3 | **Disclose LLMs, and say they can be wrong** | "Pepper's answers come from an AI language model and can be wrong." | "Pepper knows the answer to anything." |
| 4 | **Privacy at demos.** A sign at the station states whether audio or video is recorded and where it goes. Never record minors' voices or images without written parent or guardian consent | "This demo does not record audio." / "Audio is sent to [provider] to generate answers and is not stored by the lab." (only if true) | Recording visitors by default |
| 5 | **No safety or reliability promises.** State the conditions tested instead | "Tested [in the lab, at walking speed, with a person nearby]." (the conditions actually tested) | "Safe around children", "never makes mistakes", "100% reliable" |
| 6 | **No AGI, sentience or job-loss framing** | "Robots that help people rather than replace them" | "The robot is becoming conscious", "Will robots take your job?" |
| 7 | **Third-party models and services are facts, not endorsements.** Name them as `[model/provider]` only when the name is needed and confirmed | "Built with [model/provider]." | "Powered by [provider]", logos or praise that read as an endorsement by Kent State |
| 8 | **Generative AI in the lab's own marketing.** It may draft copy and make clearly labeled illustrations; a person verifies every fact before publishing. Never synthetic quotes, testimonials, voices or likenesses of real people | A drafted paragraph checked line by line against references/brand-foundation.md §9 | An invented student quote; an AI voice that sounds like the director; an AI image passed off as a lab photo |
| 9 | **Study participants in media only with IRB-approved consent.** If footage or photos come from a human-subjects study, the consent form must cover that use; ask the PI | A study photo in a news post, after the PI confirms the consent form covers publicity use | Using study session footage in a promo video because it looks good |

Checks for these rules are part of sweep 8 (Integrity) in §7. Image-side rules for AI illustrations: references/ai-image-pipeline.md and references/imagery.md.

## 5. Headlines and calls to action

### 5.1 Headline formulas

Rules: sentence case; an active verb; what is new; no jargon; about 12 words or fewer for news; numerals in headlines; a question only if the body answers it.

| Use | Formula | Example (brackets = fill with real facts) |
|---|---|---|
| Research news | [System] lets [people] [do something] [where / under what conditions] | "[System] lets [operators] [do task] from [distance / setting]" |
| Research news | Kent State researchers [build / find] [thing] that [careful outcome] | "Kent State researchers build a delivery robot that shows where it's going" (confirm with the project lead) |
| Proof-led | [Venue] [accepts / publishes] Kent State study on [topic] | "HRI 2026 Companion publishes Kent State work on readable robot motion" |
| Program | Build [what] with [real thing]. [Program], [dates] | "[Build robots that sense and move]. Summer workshop for middle school students, [dates]" (the activity stays bracketed until the program plan is published) |
| Program | The [program] for [audience] | "The robotics research internship for high school students" |
| Recruiting | [Outcome] without [common fear] | "Get research experience without having done research before" (only if the program truly requires none) |
| Sponsor | Put [your technology] in the hands of [N] student researchers | N from real records only |
| Award | [Recipient] receives [exact award name] from [body] | see §6.7 |

**The "Now [audience] can" test.** Put "Now [audience] can…" in front of the line. If the result is concrete, true and wanted, keep it. "Now high school students can do real robotics research this summer" passes. "Now you can explore the frontiers of innovation" fails.

**Hero structure (Human Action Model).** The reader's current discomfort, in their words → a better picture → one path to act. For a parent: "Another summer of screens?" → "Your child [builds and programs a robot] in a university lab" → "Register for [dates]".

### 5.2 Calls to action

Formula: **[action verb] + [what they get] + [qualifier if needed]**. Never "Submit", "Learn more", "Click here", "Get started" or "Read more" on their own. One primary call to action per piece; put the date in it when there is a deadline.

| Audience | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Parents | Sign up | Register for the [year] summer workshop |
| Teachers | Contact us | Request a lab visit for your class |
| Undergraduates | Learn more | Come to open lab on [day] |
| Graduate applicants | Apply | See open research topics |
| High school students | Apply now | Apply for the [year] internship by [date] |
| Sponsors | Contact us | Book a lab visit |
| Peers | Read more | Read the paper / Get the code |

### 6.1 Project one-liner

**Formula:** [Project] lets [who] [do what] by [how], so [outcome].

Rules: 25 words or fewer; a person doing something; one "how"; no stacked buzzwords; the project lead approves the wording, and then every channel uses the same line.

Produce four lengths at once and reuse them everywhere:

| Length | Use |
|---|---|
| Tag, 7 to 10 words | Slide subtitle, station card, social hook |
| One-liner, 25 words or fewer | Project card, caption, poster subtitle |
| 50-word blurb | Web card, program booklet |
| 150-word plain summary | Project page intro, newsletter, UCM submission |

**Worked example (VendoBot).** Source: the paper in the HRI 2026 Companion proceedings describing "A Projection-Augmented Delivery Robot for Improving Intent Transparency and Motion Legibility in Service Robots".
- Tag (8 words): "A delivery robot that shows where it's going."
- One-liner (24 words): "VendoBot is a delivery robot that uses projection to show people nearby what it plans to do, so [they can move around it confidently]."
- The bracketed outcome is a plain-language reading of "intent transparency and motion legibility"; the project lead confirms it or supplies the real one.

**Past projects** get a date: "ImmersiFLY (2017 to about 2019) was the lab's VR pilot-training simulator project, pairing a VR headset and a realistic control panel with a real drone." Describe what a past project was, not outcomes the source only states as goals (the homepage describes ImmersiFLY as a goal: what "a student pilot will be able to" do).

### 6.2 Abstract to plain language

A method for turning a paper, poster or grant abstract into public copy.

1. **Find the one sentence.** Locate "we show that…" or "our results indicate…". If there are three contributions, pick the one a non-expert can picture.
2. **Name who it matters to**, only if the paper supports it.
3. **Translate the terms** with §4.2; define any term you keep.
4. **Structure it as And, But, Therefore (ABT):** "[Context] and [context], but [problem], therefore [what we did and found]."
5. **Add one concrete image or analogy**, and have the author confirm it is accurate.
6. **Calibrate the claim.** Keep every hedge that matters: sample size ("in a lab study with [N] participants"), setting ("in simulation", "in the lab"), maturity ("preliminary"). Say what is next.
7. **Produce the four lengths** from §6.1.

**Worked example (VendoBot, structure only; results stay bracketed until the authors supply them):**

> Service robots are starting to share hallways and shops with people, and people need to know what a robot will do next. But a robot's plans are hard to read from its movement alone. So researchers in Kent State's Advanced Telerobotics Research Lab built VendoBot, a delivery robot that uses projection to show its intentions, and tested [how: study design, N participants]. [Finding, with the paper's own hedge.] Next, the team plans to [next step].

Translation notes: "projection-augmented" became "uses projection to show its intentions"; "intent transparency" became "know what a robot will do next"; "motion legibility" became "hard to read from its movement".

### 6.5 Writing about minors

The K-12 programs are the lab's most frequent minors case, and not every young participant is obviously a minor: the 2025 internship was open to "Students with general programming experience", so ages vary. Apply this checklist to any copy, caption, alt text or post that involves K-12 students, interns or their families. Policy background: Kent State Policy 3342-5-19 (references/kent-state-compliance.md); program logistics: references/pr-events-outreach.md.

- [ ] **Age unknown? Treat the person as a minor.**
- [ ] **No names** of minors in public copy, captions or alt text. (On-site name badges are a separate, event-safety matter.)
- [ ] **No identifying combinations:** no school name next to a face, and no age combined with other identifying details (hometown, school, team role).
- [ ] **No tagging:** never tag, @mention or repost a minor's own social account.
- [ ] **Quotes:** quote a minor only with written parent or guardian consent to that specific quote; quote a parent only with that parent's written consent. Never paraphrase a minor into a testimonial.
- [ ] **Credit work to the group:** "a team of high school interns built…", not a named student.
- [ ] **Photos and video:** a signed parent or guardian release on file for every minor shown (references/kent-state-compliance.md).

One-on-one messages to a participant only for a program purpose, from the program's kent.edu address, never by social DM, with a parent or guardian and a second authorized adult copied (lab practice, stricter than the policy text).
- [ ] **Supervision and safety claims** only after the program is registered with Compliance and Risk Management (at least 60 days before minors first take part) and every authorized adult's background check and training are on file. Then quote the ratio for the right age group.
- [ ] **Voices and AI demos:** no recording of minors' voices or images at demos without written parent or guardian consent (§4.4 rule 4).

### 6.9 Crediting sponsors, partners and funders

Credit lines are where a lab most easily implies more than exists: a formal partnership, a Kent State endorsement or a current relationship that ended years ago. Pick the verb that matches the actual arrangement.

| The arrangement | Write | Never write |
|---|---|---|
| A gift of money or equipment | "with support from [Company]" | "sponsored by", "powered by" |
| A sponsorship agreement made through the Office of Sponsored Programs or the Division of Philanthropy and Alumni Engagement | "sponsored by [Company]" | "sponsored by" without that agreement |
| Joint research | "in collaboration with [Organization]" | "partnered with" for a single co-authored paper |
| A formal partnership agreement | "in partnership with [Organization]" | "official partner of Kent State" |
| Past support | Date it: "[Company] supported the lab's 2018 World Robot Summit entry" | Present tense for support that has ended |
| A grant | The funder's exact name and award number, plus any required disclaimer (§3.6) | "the lab's $[total] grant" |

Rules:
- **No endorsement.** Kent State: "Do not use Kent State LOGOS for ENDORSEMENTS" (kent.edu/ucm/social/guide-social-media). Never word a credit so that Kent State appears to endorse a product, service or company. Confirm the arrangement (and who cleared it) before any credit line uses "sponsored by" or "in partnership with".
- **Names and logos exactly as the sponsor uses them.** Use the company's own spelling and capitalization, and its logo only from its official files, with written approval (references/logo-system.md for logo walls and sizing).
- **Individual donors:** named only with written consent; gift amounts never without it. Company names follow the rule in references/brand-foundation.md §9.
- **Placement:** on the web, sponsor references go in the footer of the sponsored page and take no more than 15% of the page (kent.edu/ucm/web-team/external-site-requirements). In print and on slides, the Kent State logo sits lower right and partner logos lower left (references/kent-state-compliance.md).
- **Funders:** NSF credit follows §3.6 (acknowledgment, disclaimer and logo). Show a funder's logo only when its rules or template allow it; NASA's insignia and logotype need explicit permission.
- **Thank-yous** say concretely what the support made possible and show students with the equipment or result (references/pr-events-outreach.md).

## 7. The copy-editing sweep

Edit in separate passes, one dimension per pass. The first seven sweeps adapt the Seven Sweeps method from the marketing library; the last three are ones a university lab cannot skip.

| # | Sweep | The question | Lab checks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Clarity** | Can the intended reader understand every sentence on first read? | Jargon defined or replaced (§4.2); one idea per sentence; sentences mostly 25 words or fewer; acronyms spelled out on first use; pronouns unambiguous |
| 2 | **Voice and style** | Does it sound like the lab and follow Kent State style? | §1 voice; §3 rules (no Oxford comma, "and" not "&", "Kent State University" then "Kent State", no "KSU", lab name then "the lab", AP titles, dates and times); tone matches §2 |
| 3 | **So what** | Does every claim answer "why should this reader care"? | Each feature bridged with "which means…" to an outcome for this reader |
| 4 | **Prove it** | Is every claim backed? | Every venue, date, award, number and quote traces to references/brand-foundation.md §9 or a cited source; no "first", "only", "leading" without evidence; quotes approved in writing |
| 5 | **Specificity** | Is it concrete? | Robot names, dates, times, grades, costs, places; cut what cannot be made specific |
| 6 | **Human connection** | Does a real person or scene appear? | A named person (with consent) or a concrete detail; no fear appeals, no fake urgency |
| 7 | **Clear next step** | Is it obvious what to do next? | One call to action; logistics answered; safety and supervision for K-12 (conditional); accommodations contact; what happens after they act |
| 8 | **Integrity** | Could anything mislead, even by implication? | No fabricated facts; results not upgraded (§6.7); AI claims follow §4.4; AI images captioned "Illustration" and never shown as real lab photos, robots or results; video labeled for speed (e.g. "2× speed") and for teleoperated, autonomous or scripted; simulation labeled; hedges intact; third-party images licensed and credited |
| 9 | **Accessibility and inclusion** | Can everyone use it? | §8 and §9: alt text, captions, descriptive links, heading order, no meaning by color alone, no long caps, inclusive terms |
| 10 | **Compliance** | Could it cause trouble for the lab or the university? | Minors (§6.5: release on file, no names, no tagging, age unknown means minor); student names only with written permission; funding acknowledgment and disclaimer; sponsor credit worded to match the arrangement (§6.9), names and logos approved in writing; embargo respected; invention disclosure checked before a public reveal; no athletics marks; forbidden details absent (references/brand-foundation.md §11 "Never print") |

After sweep 10, quickly re-run sweeps 1 to 3: compliance and accessibility edits often add words that blur clarity.

**Pre-publication checklist**
- [ ] Goal, audience and the single desired action written at the top of the draft
- [ ] Every fact appears in references/brand-foundation.md or has a cited source
- [ ] All ten sweeps done
- [ ] No `[placeholder]` left in anything that will be published
- [ ] Links work, are descriptive and carry tracking parameters where tracked
- [ ] The director (or project lead) approved research claims; UCM approved anything UCM will publish

**Quick pass**
- Words: cut intensifiers (very, really, extremely) and filler (just, actually, basically); "in order to" becomes "to"; turn nominalizations back into verbs ("make a decision" becomes "decide").
- Sentences: one idea each; vary the length; front-load the important word.
- Paragraphs: one topic; two to four sentences on the web.

**Plain-English swaps**

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| utilize, leverage | use |
| facilitate | help, make possible |
| demonstrate | show |
| prior to / subsequent to | before / after |
| a large number of, numerous | many |
| in the event of | if |
| commence / terminate | start / end |
| approximately | about |
| methodology (meaning method) | method |
| novel | new, or say what is new |
| in terms of, with respect to | about, for |

**Expert panel (high-stakes pieces only):** for a press release, the homepage, a sponsor prospectus, a K-12 registration page or a recruiting page, score the draft 1 to 10 from each relevant perspective (for example a skeptical science journalist, a parent of a 12-year-old, a sponsored-programs officer, an accessibility reviewer, the director for accuracy) and revise until every score is 7 or more.

## 8. Inclusive language

- **Gender-neutral by default:** "chair", "people and machines", "crewed/uncrewed", "person-hours", "staff". Singular "they" is fine for a person whose pronouns are unknown or who uses it.
- **Disability:** person-first by default ("people with disabilities", "veterans with disabilities"); identity-first when the person or community prefers it. "Uses a wheelchair", not "confined to a wheelchair"; not "suffers from". The lab's Virtual Reality Tour work was for "people with intellectual and developmental disabilities": keep that phrasing.
- **Demographics** only when relevant and with consent. Recruiting describes the work and the requirements, not who may apply (Ohio Senate Bill 1; check older diversity wording from the lab site with UCM before reuse).
- **International readers:** avoid idioms and sports metaphors ("slam dunk", "hit it out of the park"); spell names exactly as the person does, hyphens and order included ("Jong-Hoon Kim").
- **Ages and school levels:** "students ages 12 to 14", "high school students", "your child" (to parents). "Kids" only in kid-facing copy.
- **Robots and roles:** robots are "it"; "leader-follower" rather than "master-slave" teleoperation.
- **Tone about technology:** no fear framing, no violent metaphors ("attack the problem", "killer app").

| ATR logo | "Advanced Telerobotics Research Lab logo" (name it; don't describe the artwork) |
| Kent State wordmark | "Kent State University" |
| AI illustration `assets/illustrations/illus-telepresence.png` | "Illustration: an operator wearing a VR headset controls a distant robot arm over a network." (always start with "Illustration") |
| Chart | The takeaway, not the geometry: "Bar chart: [finding in one sentence]." Put the data in a table nearby (references/data-visualization.md) |
| Decorative pattern, hazard band, background texture | Mark as decorative (empty alt) |
| Graphic with text on it | Repeat the text: "Recruiting graphic: 'Build real robots before you graduate.' Photo of [description]." |
| QR code | "QR code to [destination]; the address [clean URL] is printed below it." |
| Photo with minors | No names, no school: "Middle school students test a robot they built." (release on file) |

**Plain language.** Hit the reading level for the audience (§2); average 25 words or fewer per sentence; define acronyms on first use; put the important word first; use headings that say what the section is about. Check with a readability tool before publishing K-12 and parent copy.

**Links.** Link text says where it goes: "Register for the summer workshop", "Read the paper". Never "click here", "here", "read more" or "link". No link shorteners; under every QR code, print the clean address.

**Structure.** Use real heading styles in order (not bold text posing as a heading); lists as real lists; unique slide titles; one idea per slide.

**Social specifics.** CamelCase hashtags (#HumanRobotInteraction, #KentState) at the end of the post; emojis sparingly and at the end of a sentence, never in place of words (screen readers read their names); no "fancy" Unicode letter styles; account tags at the end. Details: references/social-media.md.

**Video.** Captions on every video (review auto-captions); narrate what matters visually ("the gripper closes on the cup"); label playback speed and whether the robot is teleoperated, autonomous or scripted, in the post as well as in the video.

**Don't rely on color, position or device in words.** "Tasks marked Late", not "tasks in red"; "the table below" only when the table directly follows; "select", not "click".

## 10. How to deliver copy

When writing copy for someone:
1. State the goal, the audience and the one action at the top.
2. Deliver the copy organized by section (headline, subhead, body, call to action; or slide by slide).
3. Annotate key choices with the principle behind them (one line each).
4. Give two or three alternatives for headlines and calls to action, each with a one-line rationale.
5. For web pages, include the page title and meta description.
6. For images, include the alt text.
7. End with a list of every `[placeholder]` and every claim that needs confirmation, so a person can clear them before publishing.
